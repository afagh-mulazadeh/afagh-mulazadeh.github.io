Doctoral work

Research

My doctoral research asked a simple question with complex implications: when politicians speak in Parliament, where does their information come from? I built a computational pipeline that detects, classifies, and traces research evidence use in UK parliamentary debate, matching speeches against briefings produced by the House of Commons Library, House of Lords Library, and the Parliamentary Office of Science and Technology (POST).

I developed the Evidence in Parliament Annotation Scheme (EPAS), a five-dimensional framework covering attribution, grounding, source type, use function, and epistemic stance. Benchmarking nine LLMs against five human annotators, I found that models and humans exhibit parallel failure patterns on interpretive dimensions (use function, epistemic stance) while converging on detection-level tasks (source type, grounding) — suggesting the difficulty is intrinsic to the phenomenon, not the instrument.

The research also produced an interactive evidence tracker, developed with UK parliamentary research services and currently in discussions for licensing as a live detection system.

Where this goes next

Open-world evidence source mapping: Extending the pipeline beyond parliamentary briefings to include social media, news, think tank reports, government policy papers, and academic publications — mapping the full landscape of sources that politicians draw on and tracing knowledge networks and influence pathways across source types.

LLMs as epistemic actors: Investigating how language models handle evidence when embedded in policy research workflows — what sources they draw on, how they assign credibility, and how their evidence-use behaviour compares to that of human politicians and policy advisors. My interview data from the UK Parliament confirms that actors across the parliamentary ecosystem are already using generative AI for evidence synthesis, creating a recursive loop in which AI-mediated evidence feeds into human decision-making.

Nature · July 2026Published
Six ways to put the public at the heart of science and policy
A multi-author commentary setting out six practical mechanisms for embedding public voice in science advice, drawing on experience from the UK, Switzerland, and the EU. It argues that participation works best when built into the structure of advisory processes rather than bolted on as late-stage consultation. Written for the institutional designers and science advisers who shape those processes, rather than for a purely academic audience.
Data for Policy 2026Accepted paper
The UK Parliament Evidence Tracker: An AI-Powered Tool for Monitoring Research Evidence Use in Legislative Debate
Introduces the evidence tracker pipeline and dashboard, demonstrating how NLP can map the flow of research evidence into parliamentary debate at scale. Shows that 91.7% of detected evidence use is unattributed, making the work of parliamentary research services overwhelmingly invisible in the public record. Being developed into a full article for Data & Policy (Cambridge University Press).
ICML 2026Accepted poster
Who Counts as Evidence-Based? Knowledge Practices in British and Swiss Parliamentary Speech
A comparative analysis of evidence-use patterns across the UK and Swiss parliaments, developed with the UZH–ETH DemocraSci team. The same detection and classification pipeline is applied to both corpora, so cross-national differences reflect parliamentary practice rather than differences in measurement. Examines how legislative design and political culture shape which sources come to count as "evidence" in debate.
NeurIPS 2026 · Datasets & BenchmarksUnder review
EPAS: A Multi-Dimensional Benchmark for Evidence Use in Parliamentary Discourse
Presents the EPAS framework: five interrelated annotation dimensions, conditional coding logic, a 200-sentence gold standard coded by five human annotators, and benchmark results across nine LLMs. Models and humans struggle on the same interpretive dimensions — use function and epistemic stance — while performing comparably on detection-level tasks such as source type and grounding. That parallel failure pattern suggests the remaining difficulty is intrinsic to interpreting evidence use, not an artefact of the annotation instrument.
Science Communication · SageUnder review
Science-Informed Claims in Swiss Parliamentary Debates
A cross-national extension applying the evidence detection methods to the Swiss Federal Assembly, co-authored with the DemocraSci team. Examines how science-informed claims are distributed across parties, chambers, and policy domains in a consensus democracy. Provides a baseline for comparing how research evidence surfaces in consensus systems against the majoritarian Westminster case.
